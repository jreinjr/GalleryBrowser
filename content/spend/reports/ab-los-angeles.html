2026-09-19 to 2026-10-31 · 251 words · imgs: 1500x1875, 500x624, 1500x1875, 1500x1875, 676x1012

Charles Dickson has been carving since he was five years old, whittling toy knives under the blankets in South Central Los Angeles, and this survey at Parrasch Heijnen compresses sixty years of that practice into one gallery. Select Works, 1966-2026 pulls from across his career — wood, bronze, concrete, metal, glass, acrylic — tracking a self-taught sculptor who came up alongside Noah Purifoy and John Outterbridge in the Watts arts scene of the late 1960s and never really left it, becoming the longtime artist-in-residence at the Watts Towers Arts Center and groundskeeper of the Towers themselves. What unites the wildly different materials in Dickson's work is a consistent visual language drawn from African sculptural tradition — masks, totemic forms, ritual tools — repurposed to catalog and honor Black life and history in Los Angeles specifically. He's best known publicly for civic commissions like the Mariposa Metro Green Line station and the Martin Luther King Jr. memorial in Watts, but this gallery presentation gets at the more intimate, studio-based side of his output: carved reliefs, assemblages, and figures that read like a private notebook kept over six decades. A show spanning 1966 to 2026 is essentially a mini-retrospective, and it's a notable get for a Boyle Heights gallery to present a Los Angeles art-world elder statesman whose work usually turns up in museums and public plazas rather than commercial spaces. Worth seeing both for the sculpture itself and as a rare chance to see this much of Dickson's range in one room.

Worth a visit for anyone curious about the current cross-traffic between painting and clay in Los Angeles.

Curated by Gary Brewer, *Earth Elements: Paint and Fire* brings together painters who have turned to ceramic sculpture and ceramic artists who infuse painterly sensibility into their three-dimensional work. The exhibition draws from the history of ceramic liberation—from George Ohr's early innovations through Peter Voulkos's abstract expressionist ceramics and the irreverent, conceptually sophisticated practice of artists like Viola Frey and Robert Arneson. Rather than remaining bound by craft traditions, the artists in this show approach ceramic material as a tabula rasa, applying a painter's intuition and freedom to the medium. Two exceptions prove revealing: Galia Linn, who began as a ceramic sculptor and later expanded into painting, and Cybele Rowe, whose monumental ceramic sculptures integrate decades of painterly sensibility into what she terms "volumetric paintings." The exhibition asserts that the boundaries between painting and sculpture, between art and craft, are increasingly fluid and that the most vital contemporary work emerges when artists move fluidly across these traditional divisions.

It's a short run, just over a week, so this is very much a catch-it-while-you-can show for anyone interested in sculpture that treats the body's history of being touched, held, or neglected as its actual subject matter.

2025-11-08 to 2026-10-11 · 279 words · imgs: 1920x1080, 2560x1707, 2560x1707, 2560x1921, 2560x1707, 1612x2560

Enrique Martínez Celaya spent decades as a physicist before turning fully to art, and that background shows in the way he treats memory here less as sentiment than as a structure to be modeled and tested. The Sextant is a scaled-down reconstruction of the modernist house his father built on Cuba's coast between 1957 and 1963 — years that bracketed the Revolution, the Missile Crisis, and the U.S. embargo, and that ended with his family waiting there, never able to leave on their own terms. Rebuilding it as an installation lets Martínez Celaya interrogate what such reconstructions can and cannot recover: the piece is explicit about the gap between the model and the real house, between a childhood remembered and a childhood lived. Housed at the Wende, a museum built around Cold War-era artifacts and ephemera from behind the Iron Curtain and beyond, the piece fits naturally into the institution's broader project of examining how ordinary lives were shaped by ideological rupture. Rather than photographs or archival objects, though, viewers get an immersive architectural surrogate — a dollhouse-scaled stand-in for a real building that, today, operates as a wedding venue, its political scars mostly invisible to the couples who rent it. What makes the work worth a visit is its refusal to resolve into either nostalgia or polemic. It sits with the discomfort of authenticity questions — is a reconstruction ever equivalent to the thing itself? — while using a deeply personal family story to gesture at the universal experience of utopian dreams curdling into everyday compromise. It's a quiet, contemplative show, best experienced slowly, and it rewards taking one of the museum's docent-led tours if the schedule allows.

Ivana Dama: Breathing Alarms
2026-04-26 to 2026-10-11 · 231 words · imgs: 2560x1440, 2560x1707, 1365x1024

New York–based artist Ivana Dama transforms the Wende Museum's East German guardhouse into an immersive sound installation powered entirely by human breath. At the center of *Breathing Alarms* is a custom air-raid siren, a device historically tied to surveillance and protection, that only sounds when visitors breathe into it. The siren's intensity modulates with each person's breath while simultaneously activating an animation of birds circling inside a bespoke aluminum zoetrope—a collision of mechanical watchfulness and organic life. Dama's research into sirens draws on her own formative experience growing up in post-communist Belgrade during the 1999 NATO bombing, when the sounds and vibrations of air-raid sirens shaped her understanding of how sound carries trauma and memory. *Breathing Alarms* elevates this personal history into a collective experience, inviting visitors to recognize their own breath as a generator of acoustic power. The work reveals how air, sound, and motion underlie the technologies we often take for granted—and how shared physical presence can transform instruments of control into instruments of connection. Developed in collaboration with MIT's Center for Bits and Atoms, the installation merges cutting-edge digital fabrication with analog mechanics, creating a deeply interactive work about perception, surveillance, and the politics of breath in a moment of environmental and social crisis. It's a rare chance to experience contemporary sound art that's simultaneously intimate, politically engaged, and revelatory about the human body's capacity to generate meaning.

Philip Martin Gallery has assembled ten painters and sculptors of very different generations and temperaments for a show built around the idea of interior light — literal and psychological. The lineup spans a late Los Angeles conceptualist (Carl Cheng), a group of mid-career West Coast painters known for pattern, color, and landscape (Holly Coulis, Pamela Jorden, David Korty, Sky Glabush), and younger or more recently arrived voices working in miniature realism and collage-inflected fantasy (Sophie Treppendahl, Aaron Morse, Sung Jik Yang), alongside the late Austrian-born painter Hubert Schmalix. Aaron Morse's contribution, a large canvas of the volcanic Red Hill cinder cone in the Coso Range, extends his ongoing interest in frontier mythology and 19th-century Romantic landscape painting, while Sophie Treppendahl's jewel-sized, candlelit domestic interiors continue the hushed, still-life intimacy that has become her signature. Pamela Jorden's webbed abstractions and Christy Matson's textile-based work (shown alongside the paintings) bring a tactile, handmade counterpoint to the more illusionistic pieces by Korty and Schmalix. Taken together, the show reads as a survey of how painters right now are thinking about atmosphere and glow — sunlight through a window, a candle on a table, the volcanic light of a desert landscape — without forcing those disparate approaches into false unity. It's a good entry point into the gallery's roster and a chance to see a late Schmalix painting alongside artists two generations his junior.
